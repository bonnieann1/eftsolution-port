# How to Choose an Energy Healer: Seven Things That Matter More Than Credentials

*Published: November 2026 | eftsolution.com*

  

  

There are more people calling themselves energy healers today than at any previous point in the field's history. Some of them are excellent. Some of them are well-meaning but undertrained. A small number are simply capitalising on a growing market without the depth or integrity the work requires.

  

For someone who is genuinely considering energy healing — perhaps for the first time, perhaps having had a previous experience that did not produce what was hoped for — this landscape is legitimately confusing. The credentials that exist in the field are inconsistently regulated, the terminology varies between practitioners, and the range of training depth behind the same title can span years of rigorous study to a weekend certification.

  

I am going to be direct about this, because I think the field is ill-served by practitioners who avoid the subject: choosing the wrong energy healer can waste your time, waste your money, and in some cases, leave you more sceptical of genuinely effective work than you were before. That outcome is not in your interest, and it is not in the field's interest either.

  

What follows is not a list of credentials to check. It is a list of qualities to assess — qualities that matter more than whether someone holds a particular certificate, and that you can begin to evaluate before you book.

  

## One: They Are Specific About What They Do and How It Works

The first thing I look for when evaluating any practitioner — in any field — is whether they can explain their approach in concrete, specific terms. Not jargon. Not vague references to energy and vibration and the universe. Specific, honest language about what the modality is, what its proposed mechanism is, and what the research base, if any, looks like.

  

A practitioner who cannot explain what they do at a level that a sceptical, intelligent person can follow is either undertrained or is relying on mystique rather than substance. Neither is a good sign. Genuine expertise produces clarity, not obscurity. The more someone actually understands their field, the more simply they can describe it.

  

This does not mean a practitioner needs to have peer-reviewed papers ready to hand. It means they should be able to say something like: "EFT works by combining tapping on acupressure points with focused awareness on a specific emotional issue — this appears to send a calming signal to the amygdala while the charged material is consciously present, which allows the nervous system to update its threat response." That is a specific, honest, verifiable claim. It is the kind of thing a properly trained practitioner can say with confidence.

  

If the explanation you receive is primarily about energy and light and frequencies without any reference to what the mechanism might actually be, ask more questions. The answers will tell you what you need to know.

  

## Two: They Are Honest About What Their Work Can and Cannot Do

The second quality to assess is intellectual honesty about limitations. A practitioner who presents energy healing as a solution for everything, or who makes guarantees about outcomes, is not someone you should work with.

  

Effective energy healing has genuine limitations. It is not appropriate as the primary intervention for acute psychiatric crises. It does not replace appropriate medical investigation and care. It is not equally effective for all people and all conditions. A practitioner who tells you it can replace your medication, cure your physical illness, or guarantee specific results within a specific timeframe is either uninformed about the research or is overpromising to sell sessions.

  

The practitioners who are most effective at this work are also the most grounded in its evidence base. They describe what the research shows accurately, without inflation. They know which findings are robust, which are recent and still replicating, and where the theoretical framework is still developing. They refer clients elsewhere when the presenting issue is outside their scope.

  

Honesty about limitations is not a weakness in a practitioner. It is a marker of genuine competence and professional integrity.

  

## Three: They Lead With Your Experience, Not Their Story

A practitioner's job is to attend to you — not to your therapeutic relationship with them, not to their spiritual journey, not to their accumulated wisdom. You.

  

In an initial consultation, notice where the practitioner's attention is directed. Are they asking questions about your experience, your specific situation, what you have already tried? Or are they primarily sharing their own story, their own transformation, their own approach? Both things can have a place in an introduction, but the ratio matters. A practitioner who spends the majority of an initial conversation on their own narrative is showing you something about where their attention tends to go in a session.

  

The most skilled practitioners I have encountered — in every discipline — are characterised by a quality of genuine interest in the person in front of them. Not interest as a therapeutic technique, but actual curiosity about this specific person's specific experience. That quality cannot be faked for long, and it is the foundation of the attunement that makes energy work effective.

  

## Four: They Have a Clear Sense of When to Refer Elsewhere

An ethical practitioner knows the edges of their competence and the edges of their scope — and they refer beyond those edges without defensiveness.

  

If you are presenting with active suicidal ideation, a practitioner should not take you on as an energy healing client without appropriate clinical support in place. If you are in an acute manic episode, energy healing is not the right first response. If your chronic pain has not been properly medically investigated, a practitioner should encourage that investigation rather than suggesting energy work as the first port of call.

  

A practitioner who takes on everything, refers nothing, and positions themselves as equipped to handle any presenting issue is practising beyond their scope. This is not caution for its own sake — it is recognition that the full range of human suffering requires the full range of available responses, and no single modality or practitioner addresses all of it.

  

Ask a potential practitioner directly: "Are there situations where you would refer someone elsewhere rather than work with them?" The answer will tell you a great deal.

  

## Five: Their Training Is Substantive and Ongoing

Not all certifications are equal, and the energy healing field has not yet established the kind of standardised professional regulation that would make credential comparison straightforward. A weekend course and a three-year training can both produce someone calling themselves an EFT practitioner or an energy healer. The depth of understanding between those two people is not equivalent.

  

What you are looking for is not a specific certificate — it is evidence of sustained, substantive training and ongoing professional development. How long has the practitioner been studying and practising? With whom? What is the structure of their continuing professional education? Do they participate in supervision — the practice of having a more senior practitioner review their case work? Supervision is standard in every regulated therapeutic profession for excellent reasons, and its presence or absence in an energy healing practitioner's practice is informative.

  

It is also worth asking about their experience with presenting issues similar to yours. A practitioner who has worked extensively with anxiety in high-achieving professionals has a different depth of relevant experience than one who has worked primarily with a general population across a wide range of issues. Neither is wrong — but the match between their experience and your presenting issue matters.

  

## Six: The First Session Tells You Something Clear

You do not have to commit to a long engagement before you know whether a practitioner is right for you. A good first session — even an initial consultation — will tell you something definitive about whether the practitioner can actually work with you.

  

Specifically: do you feel genuinely attended to? Does the practitioner demonstrate that they have understood not just what you said but what you meant? Did something shift — even subtly — in the session? Did the practitioner's presence produce in you a sense of safety that made it possible to bring more of yourself to the conversation?

  

If the answer to these questions is unclear or no, that information is worth taking seriously. Not every practitioner-client pairing is a good match, and a practitioner who is excellent for one person may not be right for another. A first session that produces nothing — no shift, no sense of being genuinely met, no feeling that the work reached anything real — is the most honest data point available.

  

Trust it.

  

## Seven: They Do Not Create Dependency

The goal of any genuine therapeutic relationship is to leave the client more resourced, more capable, and less reliant on the practitioner over time — not less. A practitioner whose client work consistently requires indefinite continuation, who discourages clients from developing their own self-practice, or who creates an emotional dependency that serves the practitioner's needs rather than the client's growth, is not working in the client's interest.

  

Effective energy healing work is typically time-limited in its intensive phase. A presenting issue with a specific presenting complaint will usually show significant progress within a handful of targeted sessions. Longer-term work is appropriate when the presenting complexity is genuinely deep — when multiple layers of pattern require time to address in sequence — but it should always be proceeding toward greater independence, not lesser.

  

Ask a potential practitioner: what does a typical engagement look like? How do you measure progress? At what point would we consider the intensive work complete? A practitioner who cannot answer these questions with reasonable clarity is either unclear about their own process or has not thought carefully about what they are working toward with their clients.

  

## A Final Word

I am aware that some of what I have described in this post applies to me, and that this creates an obvious conflict of interest in a post ostensibly designed to help you evaluate practitioners. I have tried to write it in a way that would be equally useful if you were considering working with someone other than me — because that is the intent.

  

What I would add, as the final point, is this: trust your own perception. After a consultation with a practitioner, notice what you actually feel — not what you think you should feel, not what the testimonials suggested you would feel, but what is genuinely present for you. A sense of clarity and possibility, or a sense of vague unease. A feeling of having been genuinely met, or a feeling of having been processed through a service.

  

Your nervous system is a remarkably accurate instrument for detecting genuine presence and genuine competence. It has been doing this far longer than you have been consciously aware of doing it. When you consult it honestly, it tends to know what it knows.

  

That knowledge is worth listening to. It is, in a sense, what energy healing is designed to restore your access to.
